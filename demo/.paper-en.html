When the arm gets stuck,
does the model know?

A VLA keeps failing in simulation: the arm freezes mid-air, or circles between the two pots until time runs out. Its MoE router writes a log at every control step that nobody reads. We read it — and the failure turns out to be visible in that log, early enough that something can still be done about it.

routing alarm → resample in place → rescue

A trajectory is what the model did. The routing is what state it was in.

The one idea

Failure is not a behaviour. It is a state — and the state is in the routing.

At every control step of this π0-style VLA, a router names a top-4 out of 32 experts. While the arm is working, that roster keeps turning over with the observation; the moment the arm stalls, the observation freezes — and so does the roster. So there is no need to watch pixels or add sensors. Count how much of this step's roster overlaps with the last step's, and the failure shows itself.

01

The log is free

Expert selection is a by-product of the MoE forward pass — the model cannot run without making the choice. Reading it adds no inference, no probe, no instrumentation.

02

One ratio is enough

Routing change over the last 4 steps ÷ the rollout's own first 8. Dimensionless — so a single fixed threshold works across scenes and across corpora, with no per-group calibration.

03

The alarm cashes out

The alarm step is not a verdict, it is a trigger: save state, resample, and a doomed rollout comes back. Detection closes into intervention for the first time.

How the log is read

Three steps: set overlap → self-normalise → persistence.

The readout is kept deliberately dumb — no learning, no calibration, four free parameters in total. Each step has a graveyard behind it: every smarter variant we tried lost to it.

  1. top-4

    Count set overlap, ignore expert identity

    Between adjacent control steps, each (layer × token) site compares its two top-4 rosters: 1 − |∩| / |∪|, averaged over HB layers 12–15 × 10 action tokens. Renumber all 32 experts and not one digit changes — the score asks "is it still the same crew", never "which crew". That is not hygiene: the static identity recovers the initial scene at 99.6–100% accuracy, yet is worth only +2.2 points on outcome once groups are held out strictly. Identity is a scene fingerprint. Change is state.

  2. r(t)

    Divide by the rollout's own opening

    r(t) = mean of the last 4 distances ÷ mean of the own first 8. The denominator comes from the same rollout, so r is dimensionless — the threshold needs no per-scene tuning. This one step is the entire reason the threshold transfers across corpora (Question Two).

  3. K=3

    Three consecutive steps or nothing

    r(t) jitters ±10% around 1.0; even successful rollouts spend 9% of their steps below θ. A single crossing means nothing. Three consecutive steps below θ = 0.95 fire the alarm — turning a nominal 25% crossing rate into an observed 5–16% false-alarm rate.

Full configuration: HB 12–15 · denoise 9 · action tokens · top-4 · W=4 · W₀=8 · K=3 · θ=0.95. These constants were frozen on two corpora; every experiment below — including the rescue — ran with them untouched.

In the order you would ask them

A readout has to clear five bars, in order.

A readout that sees nothing is a decoration; one you cannot obtain never ships; an alarm that changes no decision is a dashboard nobody reads; and a result that will not state its own limits should not be believed even when it clears the rest.

  1. OneDoes it see failure? Two independent corpora, 352 + 512 rollouts: 80.1% / 82.0% correct (baselines 66.8% / 57.8%).
  2. TwoIs the reading really free? The log exists natively — and one shared threshold lands within 2 points of the same false-alarm rate on both corpora.
  3. ThreeWhat can be done at the alarm? Save state, swap the noise: 3 of 8 resamples rescue; live, a 52-step failure becomes an 8-step success.
  4. FourWhat does it actually mark? Stalled — not doomed. 19 of 203 alarms later recovered on their own, and they really had stalled.
  5. FiveWhat is it not? No early warning; cannot tell rescuable from hopeless; cannot pick which candidate to run. Each nailed by an experiment.

One · does it see failure

Draw all 352 branches at once and the split is visible to the eye.

Corpus A is a deliberately awkward construction: 22 saved simulator states, each fanned into 16 branches that differ only in their flow-noise stream. Same start, same task, only the noise changes — 117 succeed, 235 fail — and the routing pulls the two crowds apart.

80.1%corpus A correct (352 rollouts, baseline 66.8%)
82.0%corpus B correct (512 rollouts, baseline 57.8%)
78.3% / 16.2%A: recall / false alarms
81.5% / 17.6%B: recall / false alarms

The decision window sits inside the equal-opportunity interval where every branch is still alive (A: τ<37, B: t<35) — each branch gets exactly the same number of chances to trip. Open the window to the horizon and the same detector inflates to 98%: those extra points are the free bit "it has not ended yet", not routing. Every number on this page stays inside the clean window.

Two · is the reading really free

The log is free — and so is the threshold: one θ, two corpora.

"Free" has two layers. The shallow one: expert selection is a by-product of the forward pass, so recording it costs nothing. The deep one: because r(t) divides by the rollout's own opening, the threshold is a dimensionless number — set θ on corpus A, carry it unchanged to corpus B, and the false-alarm rates line up bracket by bracket. That is the kind of free that deploys.

One more question belongs here: the top-4 roster contains expert numbers — is identity being smuggled in? We split the matched Hellinger distance in two to check: shape only (sorted profile, no identity at all) scores 78.1%, identity permutation only scores 74.4%, together 80.1%. The signal is mostly "how concentrated the routing became"; identity is worth about two points — and the set-overlap score, invariant to renumbering, takes the former.

The point is knowing when. Without the detector you either fan out eight branches at every step (compute explodes) or you let it die. The alarm compresses the cost of intervening to a single moment. The live run is n = 1; the 8-way resample from the same state (3/8) is its distributional backdrop.

Four · what it actually marks

Stalled — not doomed. The false alarms are evidence of that, not a flaw in it.

Of 203 alarms, 19 land on rollouts that ultimately succeed. Chase them down and they are the slow successes: median length 48 steps against 39 overall. They really did stall — they just walked out of it later. The detector read "this one has stopped", and that sentence was true of them too.

We tried to push the false alarms down: longer persistence, deeper thresholds, monotonicity, cross-slice conjunctions inside the tensor (front block, other denoise rounds, the state token all collapsing at once). Every point of false alarms costs two to four points of recall, and no constraint beats plain "three consecutive steps" on overall accuracy. Not because the constraints were badly designed: under a change-rate reading the routing tensor has roughly one degree of freedom, so there is no second independent signal to conjoin — and the "false alarms" being suppressed are genuine stalls.

From the intervention side this is the right semantics anyway: acting on a struggling-but-eventually-successful rollout does no harm — those 19 also rescue under resampling. The alarm means: intervene now. It does not mean: already dead.
